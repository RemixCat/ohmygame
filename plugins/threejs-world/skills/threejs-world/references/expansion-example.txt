## Toon-Shaded Cherry Blossom Station — Three.js World Prompt

Design a richly detailed cherry blossom railway-station world with the mood of a Japanese youth anime. The setting is a quiet suburban town in Japan. Use a semi-realistic anime style: the architecture should be structurally believable while its shapes are simplified and beautified. The image should feel fresh, warm, and clear, with soft spring-afternoon light, a pale blue sky, thin clouds, and many pink petals in the air. The world needs clear spatial layers, abundant signs of everyday life, and a complete environment suitable for free exploration.

### 1. Overall spatial layout

The scene is centered on a small ground-level tram or local railway station. The tracks cross the town laterally, with the station placed slightly behind the middle of the composition. One side connects to a residential street and a shopping street; the other opens toward a quieter housing area, a riverbank, or a planted slope.

Before fixing coordinates, establish the larger terrain relationship: low mountains lie to the north, the station sits on a central gentle terrace, an eastern river valley provides a crossing, and residential houses climb the western slope. Roads, river channels, banks, bridges, and local terrain are designed together and may be revised together. The semantic terrain image, viewed references, world direction, scale, station, streets, river crossing, roads, and review views all inform the same spatial plan.

The main street extends from the foreground to the station entrance and forms a clear visual guide. It should bend slightly and rise or fall gently instead of being perfectly straight, allowing distant buildings to create natural occlusion and depth. Low-rise homes, a convenience store, grocery shops, a café, a wagashi shop, a flower shop, a small bookstore, a bicycle shop, and traditional Japanese storefronts line both sides.

Buildings should not all share one world-axis rotation. Street-facing buildings may follow the road, riverfront buildings may turn toward the water or follow the river bend, corner buildings may answer an intersection, and hillside homes may rotate with the contour and entrance elevation. Vary setbacks, spacing, courtyards, and entry relationships while keeping the street and public routes readable.

Create a modest station forecourt with a waiting area, bicycle parking, vending machines, a public telephone, a post box, a bus stop sign, a neighborhood map, benches, planters, and a station-name sign. Keep a continuous clear route through the forecourt.

The railway corridor needs a complete setting: one or two tracks, ballast, sleepers, signals, utility poles, overhead cables, a crossing, fences, railway warning signs, trackside equipment boxes, drainage, and scattered weeds.

The river valley and its crossing belong to the same spatial system. Shape the riverbed, shallow margins, banks, bridge approaches, and roads together. Keep the main roads and pedestrian routes continuous from the town to the bridge, through the forecourt, and to the opposite neighborhood.

### 2. Street details

Use the narrow two-way or single-lane roads common in suburban Japan. Their faded dark-gray asphalt should show small patches, hairline cracks, drainage traces, and light tire wear rather than a perfectly clean surface.

Paint white edge lines and occasional orange or yellow center lines. Near the station and shops, add white Japanese road markings such as “止まれ,” speed warnings, pedestrian-priority marks, and bicycle symbols.

Sidewalks are narrow. Some blocks have no separate sidewalk and use the road edge line to mark the pedestrian strip. Add continuous or broken concrete drainage channels covered by metal grates or concrete lids. A few petals and leaves collect around the grates.

Place convex traffic mirrors at corners with a slight curved reflection. Intersections may have blue direction signs, speed-limit signs, no-parking signs, utility-pole number plates, and school-route signs.

Scatter a restrained set of everyday objects: bicycles and commuter bikes with front baskets; plastic crates, cardboard boxes, chalkboard menus, and folding signs outside shops; umbrella stands, potted plants, watering cans, and recycling bins at homes; a small white van, kei car, or vintage taxi; reflective strips, yellow collision guards, and small stickers at utility poles; petals gathered by wind; leaves, manholes, fire-hydrant markings, and maintenance patches.

Buildings, foundations, steps, decorations, and environmental assets must stay out of the road's clear passage. A road must remain physically traversable and connect entrances, the station, the bridge, the forecourt, and the opposite neighborhood. Do not treat a painted strip as a usable route if an object blocks it.

### 3. Buildings along the streets

Use one- to three-story Japanese low-rise buildings rather than modern towers. Vary their massing and roof heights to create a natural small-town skyline.

#### 3.1 Japanese homes

Most homes are two-story detached houses. Exterior finishes may include light beige stucco, white paint, pale blue siding, light-gray tile, or dark brown timber. Roofs use dark gray, blue-gray, or deep green Japanese tiles; some homes have simple metal pitched roofs. Give eaves visible thickness, gutters, and downspouts leading toward street drainage.

Use sliding windows with dark gray or brown frames. Some windows have frosted glass, blinds, rain guards, or pale curtains. Narrow second-floor balconies may hold white shirts, school uniforms, towels, and sheets.

Entrances have small genkan canopies, house plates, mailboxes, doorbells, shoe mats, and warm porch lights. Place ceramic pots, greenery, hydrangeas, dwarf pines, nandina, or small bonsai near the doors. Some homes use low concrete walls, wooden lattice, wire fencing, or clipped hedges. Corners may show moss, rain stains, and tiny cracks while remaining clean and welcoming.

#### 3.2 Traditional shops

Add one or two Showa-era shops with timber or stucco walls and dark wood-framed sliding glass doors. Hang fabric noren curtains with simple Japanese shop names. Under the eaves, place small lightboxes, wooden signs, paper lanterns, or handwritten business notices.

Shop windows display sweets boxes, tea tins, ceramics, magazines, or retro toys. Glass has a soft reflection and carries opening hours, payment marks, and seasonal posters. The wagashi shop may show cherry-blossom mochi, a wooden display rack, a red bench, and a short bamboo screen. The grocery shop may place drink cases, rice bags, brooms, buckets, and plastic baskets outside.

#### 3.3 Convenience store

Use a white or light-gray modern exterior with automatic glass doors. A bright but restrained horizontal sign sits above the entrance; avoid real brands. Through the windows, show shelves, refrigerated cases, a register, magazines, and warm interior lights. Add posters for new drinks, boxed meals, ice cream, and seasonal campaigns.

Outside, place recycling bins, an umbrella rack, an ATM sign, bicycle parking lines, and two vending machines. The entrance is slightly above the street and includes an accessible ramp and yellow tactile paving.

#### 3.4 Café or dessert shop

Design a small Japanese café with cream stucco and dark brown timber frames. Large windows reveal wooden tables, pendant lamps, plants, coffee equipment, and a dessert case. Use a dark-green, burgundy, or warm-white awning. A handwritten blackboard lists cherry-blossom lattes, strawberry cake, and red-bean toast. Place small pots, glass bottles, and menu cards on the sill, with two round tables and metal chairs outside.

#### 3.5 Flower shop

Fill a small flower-shop entrance with wooden crates, metal buckets, and display stands. Flowers may include tulips, daisies, baby's breath, roses, hydrangeas, and cherry branches. Use a pale green or warm-white exterior, a small wind chime, handwritten bouquet prices, wrapping paper, ribbons, scissors, and vases.

### 4. Utility poles and overhead lines

Place many orderly Japanese concrete utility poles. Their gray surfaces carry light weathering, number plates, black-and-yellow warning bands, and metal brackets. Mount transformers, distribution boxes, insulators, lamps, traffic signs, small security cameras, and an intricate but organized bundle of cables.

Many wires cross overhead, creating a strong small-town silhouette. Give them natural sagging arcs instead of perfectly straight lines. Some cables branch toward house walls. Near the station, add taller railway power poles and catenary so street wiring and the railway system form distinct layers.

### 5. Station building

The station is a small suburban ground-level station with a simple, warm, slightly aged character. Use pale beige, light gray, or soft yellow walls and a dark-gray metal pitched roof with gutters. Above the entrance, hang a station-name sign using Japanese kanji and hiragana with a small English romanization.

The entrance may be open or use sliding glass doors. Yellow tactile paving connects it to the forecourt, ticket area, and platform. Near the entrance include a line map, timetable, fare table, neighborhood map, ticket gates, automated ticket machines, an IC-card reader, a lost-and-found notice, no-smoking signs, a staff window, a noticeboard, an umbrella rack, recycling bins, and a wall clock.

Ticket machines may have pale-blue, gray, or green housings, route-selection screens, coin and bill slots, a ticket outlet, and a change tray. Use two to four gates, with an optional traditional staffed window. Post spring-tourism posters, railway-safety notices, local festival announcements, a cherry-viewing map, and student drawings inside.

### 6. Platform details

Use pale-gray concrete paving with yellow tactile warning tiles along the edge. Paint a white safety line and mark waiting positions at the doors. Show slight wear, cracks, rain marks, patch-color differences, petals, and dust.

Add long benches in dark green, blue, or brown, with recycling bins, a vending machine, platform-number signs, and a station clock. The waiting canopy uses slender metal posts and a pale-gray or translucent roof. Hang fluorescent lights, line-direction signs, station-name signs, and timetables underneath.

Use a classic railway sign with the large station name in the center, previous and next stations at the sides, a white background, dark-blue text, and a soft pink line color. Platform fences may be white railings, green wire mesh, or a light-gray concrete wall, with weeds, dandelions, daisies, and low shrubs outside. At the platform end, add a no-entry sign, a small signal, track equipment boxes, number plates, and a safety fence.

### 7. Tracks and railway equipment

Build complete steel tracks with two bright-topped rails, dark-brown or gray sleepers, metal fasteners, and dark-gray ballast. Ballast stones vary in size and include gray, dark gray, brown, and a few pale pieces. Sparse weeds grow through gaps.

Rail tops are brighter silver-gray from friction; their sides are darker with slight rust. Make joints, switches, and fastening bolts visible. Along the railway place red and green signals, kilometer markers, speed signs, track numbers, equipment boxes, cable troughs, maintenance paths, drainage, fences, emergency telephone signs, switch-control equipment, and trackside reflectors.

The overhead system uses dark-gray power poles, crossbeams, insulators, suspension wires, and contact wires. Let cables converge naturally in the distance.

### 8. Local train

Use a one- to three-car Japanese suburban commuter train with a simple, friendly anime shape. The body may be cream white, silver gray, or pale beige with a cherry-pink, light-blue, or mint-green stripe. Round the nose slightly and give it a broad front window.

The front includes headlights, red tail lights, a line number, a destination display, the driver's window, wipers, a car number, a simplified railway mark, a coupler, and lower mechanical details. Arrange side windows and sliding doors regularly. Pale blue-gray reflections reveal handrails, straps, seats, and passenger silhouettes.

Add yellow or orange safety lines and priority marks near the doors. The roof carries air-conditioning units, ventilation, a pantograph, and cables. Keep wheels and bogies visible with dark-gray mechanisms, springs, and brakes. The train may stop with doors open, while petals lift and spiral in its airflow.

### 9. Railway crossing

Place a small level crossing near the station, connecting the main street with the opposite residential area. Use rubber or concrete crossing panels where tracks pass through the road, with black-and-yellow warning poles.

Include alternating red warning lights, striped barrier arms, crossbuck signs, an alarm speaker, a pedestrian waiting line, a bicycle mark, a control box, and metal fencing. The barriers may be lowered while a train passes slowly. A student with a bicycle, pedestrians, or a small car can wait behind the line. When open, the road remains continuous on both sides.

### 10. Vending machines

Place three to five vending machines at the station entrance, platform, convenience store, and a quiet street corner. Use coordinated white, red, blue, or pale-green housings with drink displays.

Products include canned coffee, green tea, lemon soda, strawberry milk, mineral water, orange juice, a cherry limited drink, hot red-bean soup, and corn soup. Add price labels, hot/cold marks, payment slots, collection openings, dust, scratches, rain stains, recycling bins, plastic crates, advertising flags, and a petal-covered bench. Machines in shadow may emit a soft white or pale-blue glow.

### 11. Cherry blossom trees

Cherry trees are the main visual element. Distribute them at the station entrance, platform exterior, street corners, behind residential walls, and along the railway.

Trunks branch naturally and use dark brown-gray bark with rough texture and horizontal cracks. Avoid straight trunks. Canopies consist of irregular branches rather than uniform spheres. Flowers are dense but leave some branches visible.

Use almost-white pale pink, soft cherry pink, warm peach-pink, and deeper pink centers. The lit side is brighter and the backlit side shifts toward pale violet-gray. A few muted yellow-green new leaves appear among the flowers.

Large trees may lean over roads to form a natural arch. Branches partially occlude station signs, roofs, trains, and streets. Around the roots use square tree pits, low stone walls, or natural soil with grass, small flowers, and moss.

### 12. Falling petals

Many petals drift through the air with depth-dependent density. Foreground petals are larger and sharper; midground petals form trails around trees, streets, and the train; distant petals become tiny points of pink light.

Wind makes petals rotate, tumble, drift sideways, and descend in loose spirals. Some land on the train, gather in ballast, rest on benches and machines, float in shallow drainage water, stick to café windows, collect at road corners, cross the station sign, or lift in the train's airflow. Ground petals form wind-shaped bands, small piles, and scattered patches rather than an even carpet.

### 13. Station forecourt

Use pale-gray square tiles or concrete with slight color variation and small plants or dust in the joints. Place a large cherry tree near the center or edge, surrounded by a circular wooden bench with a schoolbag, shopping bag, or unfinished drink.

The forecourt includes a station map, tourist-information board, bus stop sign, taxi sign, red post box, public telephone booth, bicycle racks, flowerbeds, bollards, chain barriers, vending machines, recycling bins, and a community board. Bicycles have different colors, baskets, child seats, locks, or rain covers. Beds contain tulips, pansies, daisies, and low shrubs edged with brick or concrete. Keep the main pedestrian route clear.

### 14. Anime-style signs of life

Add a small number of story-rich human traces: a station worker, students, a girl waiting with a bicycle, a passenger reading, a café worker, an older shopper, a teenager at a vending machine, passenger silhouettes, cats, sparrows, and people whose clothes and hair lift in the breeze.

Use simple anime proportions, clear silhouettes, and low-saturation clothing so figures do not compete with the station or buildings.

### 15. Small environmental objects

Use exterior air-conditioning units, exposed pipes, meters, satellite dishes, rooftop antennas, clothes poles, wind chimes, slippers, delivery boxes, newspapers, milk crates, buckets, cleaning tools, umbrellas, traffic cones, fire-extinguisher cabinets, disaster-supply cabinets, small shrines, Jizo statues, ema racks, fox or tanuki ornaments, maneki-neko figures, capsule-toy machines, ice-cream and taiyaki flags, ramen lanterns, old festival posters, mascot stickers, railway souvenir stands, and a station stamp table.

Distribute these objects naturally. Avoid neat rows and keep the scene from becoming overcrowded. Keep all objects outside required road passages.

### 16. Materials and toon-shaded visual style

Use a polished Japanese toon-shaded style. Keep silhouettes clean and avoid excessively realistic surface complexity. Buildings, eaves, window frames, the train, and tree trunks may have restrained dark outlines.

Materials use soft, clearly separated color planes. Shadows use low-saturation blue-violet or gray-violet instead of pure black. Lit areas stay bright, clean, and transparent like an animated film.

Walls may have slight hand-painted variation, simplified stains, and soft texture, without photographic noise. Glass uses a clear blue-gray reflection while preserving interior silhouettes. Metal has restrained highlights. Wood uses simplified grain and light-dark planes. Cherry canopies use layered flower clusters with three or four pink value levels.

### 17. Color and atmosphere

The palette uses cherry pink, sky blue, cream white, light gray, pale green, and warm wood brown. Streets and tracks use neutral gray. Train stripes, shop signs, vending machines, and traffic signs provide small high-saturation accents.

The sky is clear spring blue with a few thin white clouds stretched by wind. Haze makes distant buildings and hills paler and bluer. The mood is quiet, gentle, and nostalgic, suggesting wind, flowers, a distant train, the crossing bell, and the slow daily rhythm of a small town.

### 18. Lighting

Set the time around four o'clock on a spring afternoon. Side-back sunlight creates long but soft shadows. Cherry branches cast broken shadows across roads, station walls, and the train. Train metal, rail tops, windows, and vending machines receive soft highlights.

Backlit petals have slightly glowing edges and occasional translucent centers. The canopy edge has a pale pink halo without overexposure. Keep the station canopy, shops, and house eaves softly shaded, with a little warm interior light against the cool outdoor sky.

### 19. Camera composition

Use a camera slightly below or near eye height, looking toward the station from one end of the street. The main street is a leading line connecting foreground houses, midground cherry trees, and the distant station.

The foreground may be framed by a cherry branch, bicycle, vending machine, or shop sign. The midground shows the forecourt, crossing, and waiting figures. The background shows the train, platform, tracks, overhead lines, and houses on the distant slope.

The station, train, and large cherry tree form the main visual triangle. Signs, overhead lines, road markings, and tracks reinforce perspective. The image should feel like an animated-film still: rich in detail but with a clear center and intentionally varied information density.
